Net Zero technology will bring sounds we’ve never lived with into our towns and cities: electric cars, delivery drones, air taxis and heat pumps. I research how these noise sources affect us, before they arrive.

Noise is a “silent killer”, second only to air pollution in its burden on health in Europe1. The next wave of technology will introduce noise that loudness alone cannot accurately judge2: the high-pitched whine of a rotor, the tonal hum of a compressor, rumbles of large aircraft3,4. My PhD research, sponsored by the Department for Environment, Food and Rural Affairs (Defra), seeks to accurately simulate these future soundscapes to gather response data from listening panels, for machine learning models to predict who will be annoyed, to what extent, and why. These results give policymakers & regulators evidence to set standards on, and engineers targets for quieter machines, before they roll out en masse.

The Research

  1. 01

    Soundscape

    Real places are recorded, then drones, eVTOL aircraft, heat pumps and EVs are added at realistic levels.

  2. 02

    Auralisation

    Each scene is rendered spatially, so listeners hear it as they would standing there.

  3. 03

    Listening Panels

    Participants rate annoyance in controlled jury tests, across different ambiences and contexts.

  4. 04

    Machine Learning

    Models learn which acoustic, operational and contextual factors drive the ratings.

  5. 05

    Prediction

    Annoyance can be estimated for scenarios that do not exist yet, giving regulators evidence and engineers a target.

  6. 06

    Findings

    Models trained on listener ratings predict drone and aircraft annoyance more reliably than traditional metrics alone. See the papers

The Ethos

Quieter isn’t always better. What matters is how a sound is heard: by whom, where, and alongside what. Perception-driven engineering puts listeners inside the design loop, so products are shaped by the people who’ll live with them, not just by a sound level meter.

It’s already how the best-sounding products are made. Škoda’s DuoBell, developed with acousticians at the University of Salford, rings in a narrow band that noise-cancelling headphones let through. Dyson appliances such as vacuums and hairdryers are shaped by how they sound, with Salford’s auralisation letting engineers hear new designs before they are built. Bentley has put listeners in a full-vehicle simulator to judge the wind noise inside its cabins. My research brings the same loop to drones, air taxis, heat pumps and electric vehicles, before they arrive in our streets and skies.

The Gap

At the same sound level, some sources annoy far more people than others. Wind turbines, the first Net Zero source studied at scale, sit well above road, rail and aircraft. Drones, air taxis, heat pumps and e-scooters have no agreed curves yet.
